Points of Tension

Debates at the NLP–Psychology Border

Can human psychology be mapped onto LLMs without distortion? Three families of controversy from the survey's discussion.

“Attention” in psychology means selective mental focus and executive control; in transformers it is a token-weighting mechanism with no cognitive awareness (Lindsay, 2020). “Memory” in psychology implies structured encoding and recall; in LLMs it usually means context windows or weight parameters.

Anthropomorphic descriptors of LLM behavior are measurably on the rise (Ibrahim & Cheng, 2025), shaping public and scholarly assumptions. The survey calls for a precise cross-disciplinary lexicon that disentangles metaphor from mechanism.

NLP sometimes imports concepts that are outdated or contested in psychology itself. Predictive coding is a popular analogy for next-token prediction, yet contemporary neuroscience emphasizes hierarchical, multi-scale prediction (Antonello & Huth, 2024; Caucheteux et al., 2023). MBTI persists in LLM studies despite long-standing criticism of its validity and reliability (Pittenger, 1993; McCrae & Costa, 1989).

LLM “working memory” modules don't replicate human capacity limits — raising a genuinely open question: should AI emulate human cognitive constraints for interpretability, or exceed them for performance?

RLHF echoes behaviorism's classic blind spot: optimizing observable reward while neglecting internal states, risking reward hacking (Skalse et al., 2022). And the "stochastic parrots" debate over whether LLMs understand language at all remains unresolved (Bender et al., 2021).

The flagship example: GPT-4 solves ~75% of false-belief tasks — matching a six-year-old child (Kosinski, 2024). Emergent theory of mind, or sophisticated pattern matching (Strachan et al., 2024)? Minor prompt changes can derail performance entirely (Shapira et al., 2024).

Personality research shows the same tension: some studies find stable simulated traits (Sorokovikova et al., 2024), others find prompt-dependent variability (Gupta et al., 2024) — are personas inherent or mimicked (Tseng et al., 2024)?

Psychologists studying LLMs urge stronger construct-validity checks before importing human instruments, and warn against inferring human-like cognition from surface similarity (Abdurahman et al., 2024; Blank, 2023; Cuskley et al., 2024).

What's Next

Untapped Opportunities

Four areas where the survey argues psychology could push LLM research further.

Social psychology

Group dynamics, identity, and attitude-change theories (balance theory, cognitive dissonance) are barely used. Inoculation theory (McGuire, 1964) could proactively harden systems against manipulative persuasion.

Behavioral psychology

Partial reinforcement (Ferster, 1966) and shaping are overlooked in RLHF. Reward variability could reduce premature convergence and mitigate reward hacking (Amodei et al., 2016).

Personality psychology

Current work fixates on static trait theory. Developmental accounts of how traits emerge and adapt across contexts could yield more coherent, interpretable personas than prompt-based role-play.

Cognitive psychology

Schema theory (Anderson & Pearson, 1984) — knowledge organized into experience-built schemas — could improve long-term context handling, user-history compression, and generalization.

About this site. An educational, interactive companion to the survey A Review of Incorporating Psychological Theories in LLMs by Zizhou Liu, Ziwei Gong, Lin Ai, Zheng Hui, Run Chen, Colin Wayne Leach, Michelle R. Greene, and Julia Hirschberg (EACL 2026, pp. 7459–7495). Theory definitions, exploration statuses, and cited applications are summarized from the survey and its appendix tables; consult the paper for the complete reference list.

A note on ethics — echoed from the survey's limitations: reinforcement mechanisms that make systems engaging can also condition compulsive use. The authors call for transparent disclosure of reinforcement mechanisms and professional ethical guidelines, and acknowledge psychology's own critiques (WEIRD sampling, individual-level focus).
